# Beginner ukulele sequence (StandardTune)

## Scope (new learning instrument)

Tuner profiles `ukulele-standard` and `ukulele-baritone` already exist. **No ukulele lesson path ships today.** This brief specifies **at most three** beginner lessons (`uke-*`) for a **new learning instrument**, separate from acoustic, electric, classical, and bass guitar paths. Do **not** graft these lessons into guitar units, reuse guitar chord boxes, or treat six-line guitar tab as a uke map.

**Scoring limit:** StandardTune cannot pitch-score ukulele strings today. Every lesson is **SELF-CHECK only**—ear, hand feel, and the Tune screen for pitch reference. Do not attach fake Follow Me drills, guitar-string indexes, or scored GCEA/DGBE targets that pretend the matcher supports ukulele.

**Standards arc (sequencing only; not a copy of any published course):** hold → tune → two chords → a simple strum → a plucked open-string melody → then four-line tab literacy later. This brief covers the first three milestones in three lessons. Named commercial songs, James Hill pieces, and book exercises are out of scope; use original prose and generic shapes only.

**Tunings (reference):**

| Profile / preset | Open strings (as named left → right toward the floor for a right-handed player) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ukulele-standard` · Standard High G | **G4 C4 E4 A4** | Re-entrant **high G**: the string nearest the ceiling sounds *higher* than the C beside it. Default beginner map. |
| `ukulele-standard` · Standard Low G | **G3 C4 E4 A4** | Linear **low G**: same letter names, but G sits *below* C. Chord fingerings often match high G; melody and open-string order feel different. |
| `ukulele-baritone` · Standard D | **D3 G3 B3 E4** | Baritone is **not** a guitar chart. Same letter names as a guitar’s top four strings, but four strings only—no low E/A, no six-line tab, no six-string diagrams. |

State **high-G vs low-G** in hold/tune copy. Prefer high-G wording for the first path; mention low-G as an alternate preset the learner may already own. Baritone players use the same lesson *ideas* (hold, two shapes, open pluck) with DGBE names—never “just play the guitar diagram and ignore two strings.”

**Wrong tools:** guitar chord diagrams and **six-line** guitar tab. Right tools for later work: word-described finger placements, optional four-line uke tab (A on top for soprano/concert/tenor; E on top for baritone DGBE), and the existing ukulele tuner profiles.

## Lesson `uke-hold-tune`

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **id** | `uke-hold-tune` |
| **title** | Hold Steady, Then Tune GCEA |
| **description** | Balance a ukulele without gripping the neck, name the four strings, and tune with the ukulele profile. Self-check only—no scored drill. |
| **minutes** | 5 |
| **outcome** | Support the instrument so both hands stay free, and tune each string to the chosen high-G or low-G (or baritone DGBE) preset using Tune. |
| **practice** | Sit or stand with the uke against your body. Release the fretting hand and confirm the instrument still rests. Open Tune, select Ukulele (or Baritone Ukulele), pick Standard High G unless you know you have a low-G string, and adjust one string at a time with small peg turns. Recheck all four. Judge comfort and pitch yourself. |
| **readyWhen** | The uke stays balanced with a relaxed fretting hand, you can name your open strings in order, and you know whether you are on high G, low G, or baritone DGBE. |

### Sections

#### Balance first, then free both hands

A beginner ukulele should rest against your torso so the neck is not a handle. Sit on a stable chair or stand with the body of the instrument lightly against your chest or belly; keep the headstock angled so fretting fingers can reach the first frets without hiking a shoulder. For a right-handed player the fretting hand is on the left; reverse the picture if your uke is set up the other way. Form a soft C-shape around the neck without pinching. Pluck one open string gently with a fingertip or soft nail, then take the fretting hand completely off. If the instrument slips or twists, adjust strap, posture, or how the arm cradles the body before you chase perfect pitch. Pain, numbness, or a jammed wrist means stop and rest—this path is a new learning instrument in StandardTune, not a gate inside any guitar course, and no lesson count is worth forcing a hold that hurts.

#### Name high G, low G, or baritone before you twist pegs

Open Tune and choose the profile that matches the instrument in your hands. On `ukulele-standard`, **Standard High G** is re-entrant G4–C4–E4–A4: the string nearest the ceiling is a high G that sounds *above* the neighbouring C. **Standard Low G** is G3–C4–E4–A4: same letters, but that first string is an octave lower and the open set climbs smoothly. Say the four names aloud as you pluck. If you hold a **baritone**, select `ukulele-baritone` and tune D3–G3–B3–E4—do not open a guitar preset and “skip two strings,” because guitar diagrams and six-line tab describe a different layout. Bring each string into the green with tiny turns, mute the others if the phone jumps, then cycle through all four again. The tuner gives a reference pitch; marking this lesson practised is a self-check that *you* can hold and name your tuning, not a microphone exam of ukulele accuracy.

## Lesson `uke-two-chord-strum`

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **id** | `uke-two-chord-strum` |
| **title** | Two Shapes and a Soft Downstrum |
| **description** | Form open C and G7 on high-G (or low-G) GCEA—or the matching I and V idea on baritone—then strum slow downs. Self-check only—no scored drill. |
| **minutes** | 6 |
| **outcome** | Make two clear open shapes and keep a calm downstrum pulse while changing slowly between them. |
| **practice** | On GCEA: build C (only the floor-side string fretted at 3; others open), then G7 (first-fret on the E string, second-fret on C and A as described below). Strum four soft downs on C, pause, four on G7. On baritone DGBE, use two easy open shapes that function as I and V in G (for example open G and a simple D7 described in words)—never a six-string guitar box. Listen string-by-string yourself. |
| **readyWhen** | Each shape rings on the strings you intend, you can change without a countdown, and your downstrum stays gentle enough that unused noise is obvious to your ear. |

### Sections

#### Two open shapes in words—not guitar boxes

On standard GCEA (high G or low G), start with a one-finger **C**: fretting-hand ring (or middle) presses the string nearest the floor at fret 3; leave the other three strings open. Pluck one string at a time and fix buzz before you strum. Next form **G7** as a compact three-finger shape: index on the E string at fret 1, middle on the C string at fret 2, ring on the A string at fret 2, with the G string open. These are generic I and V colours in C major—original practice shapes described for StandardTune, not diagrams traced from a book. Baritone players on DGBE should pick two equally small open shapes that serve as I and V in G (for example an open G using fingers on the E and B strings in the first frets, and a simple D7), always named for four strings. Do not paste a guitar C or G7 chart and mute two courses; that teaches the wrong map. Four-line uke tab can wait until after these shapes feel ordinary.

#### Soft downs, then a slow change

With one clear shape under your fingers, brush all four strings downward with the pad of a finger or a soft fingernail—small motion from the wrist, not a heavy arm chop. Count “one-two-three-four” and land one down per beat at a tempo where every intended string speaks. After four downs on C, release, build G7 without racing, and take four downs there. If the change collapses, mute, reform, and skip the countdown until the fretting hand remembers the path. This lesson is self-check: StandardTune will not score ukulele chord tones today, so *you* decide when both shapes are clean and the pulse survives the change. Keep volume neighbour-friendly; the goal is a usable two-chord strum on a new instrument path, not a performance or a copied song loop.

## Lesson `uke-open-pluck`

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **id** | `uke-open-pluck` |
| **title** | Open-String Melody, One Note at a Time |
| **description** | Pluck a short original melody on open strings only, listening for even tone. Self-check only—no scored drill. |
| **minutes** | 5 |
| **outcome** | Play a clear four-to-six-note open-string phrase in order, with quiet neighbours and a steady, unhurried pace. |
| **practice** | On high-G GCEA, pluck this original pattern slowly: open C, E, A, E, C, then open high G (the re-entrant string). On low G, use the same letter order knowing the first G is lower. On baritone, pluck open D, G, B, E, B, G as your open-string phrase. Pause between notes; mute strings you are not using. No fretting required. |
| **readyWhen** | You can repeat the phrase from memory at a slow count, each note starts cleanly, and you are not relying on six-line guitar tab. |

### Sections

#### One open string, then a tiny original phrase

Leave the fretting hand soft or resting so it only mutes when needed. On high-G GCEA, pluck the open C string alone and listen for a centered start without a neighbouring buzz. Add open E, then open A, returning to E and C so the ear hears a small arch—then finish on the open high G so you feel how re-entrant tuning places that “first” string above the others. Low-G players use the same letter path with a deeper final (or opening) G. Baritone players mirror the idea on open D–G–B–E without importing guitar string numbers. This phrase is original practice material for StandardTune; it is not a song title, not a licensed melody, and not an exercise lifted from a published ukulele method. If a note thuds, move the plucking finger closer to the soundhole center and lighten the attack rather than pressing imaginary frets.

#### Even tone beats speed—and tab comes after the ear

Repeat the phrase while counting a slow pulse, one note per beat or every two beats. Aim for similar volume on each string; the re-entrant high G often surprises beginners by sounding brighter or higher than expected—that awareness is part of the outcome. Keep unused strings quiet with a light fretting-hand touch. When the order is memorised, you may later sketch it as **four-line** ukulele tab (open zeroes on the correct lines)—never as six-line guitar tab with two discarded courses. Until then, names and ears are enough. Mark the lesson practised when *you* hear a calm, ordered open-string melody; the app cannot certify ukulele pitches in Learn today. These three `uke-*` lessons start a ukulele learning instrument track and remain outside guitar path progress.
