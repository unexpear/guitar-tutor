# Beginner viola sequence (StandardTune brief)

## Scope (not a path)

Tuner profile `viola` is **experimental**. Learn has **no** viola learning instrument and **no** viola path. This brief specifies **at most three** optional, short, **SELF-CHECK** lessons for a first bow session. They must not be required gates, progress prerequisites, or advertised as a complete course.

Sequencing follows common ABRSM bowed-strings beginner order (2024 syllabus as a research touchstone only): hold the instrument → bow on open strings → a few reading landmarks. Do **not** copy graded pieces, scale lists, exam prose, or published staff exercises. StandardTune’s microphone may help confirm an open pitch in the Tuner; it does **not** grade bow stroke, contact point, posture, or fingered intonation.

**Tuning (low → high):** C3 · G3 · D4 · A4 (`viola-standard`). A violin chart (G3 D4 A4 E5, treble clef) is the **wrong map**—different open pitches, different clef, larger instrument.

**Rule:** open strings before left-hand fingers. No fretting/fingered lessons in this brief.

## Recommended order (bow-first)

1. `viola-hold` — seated or standing balance
2. `viola-bow-open-cg` — bow hold + open C and G only
3. `viola-alto-landmarks` — alto-clef **names** for the four open strings (no copied staff drills)

## Lesson `viola-hold`

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **id** | `viola-hold` |
| **title** | Seated or Standing Viola Hold |
| **description** | Balance a larger instrument so neither hand props it up. |
| **minutes** | 5 |
| **outcome** | Hold the viola steadily seated or standing without gripping the neck to keep it up. |
| **practice** | Choose a stable chair without arms, or stand with feet roughly under the shoulders. Raise the instrument to the shoulder/jaw contact you use; check that the scroll points slightly left and forward, not down at the floor. Release the left hand for a few seconds while the body and jaw/shoulder keep the viola in place. If it slips, adjust height and contact—not a tighter neck grip. |
| **readyWhen** | You can let go with the left hand briefly and the viola stays balanced; shoulders and jaw feel usable, not clamped. Self-check only—the app cannot see posture. |

### Sections

#### Find a calm seated or standing frame

Viola is larger and heavier than violin, so a rushed “chin clamp” often replaces real balance. Sit forward on a firm chair with both feet free, or stand with soft knees and weight even on both feet. Bring the instrument up so the lower bout rests against your collarbone/shoulder area and the jaw finds a light, repeatable contact—use a shoulder rest or pad if that is how you play. The neck should rise slightly, giving the bowing arm room across a wider body. Keep the scroll away from your face and out of a dive toward the floor. Breathe once with both hands off the neck: if the instrument drops, fix height and contact points before you worry about the bow. This is observation and comfort, not a scored drill.

#### Free the left hand before any fingers

Early viola work fails when the left hand becomes a clamp that holds the instrument up. After the frame feels stable, place the left hand lightly on the neck as if you might play—then open the fingers and lift the hand away for a count of three. The viola should stay put. If you cannot release, lower the instrument slightly, check jaw/shoulder contact, and try again. Do not start finger patterns or violin fingering charts here: those assume a different size and a different set of open pitches. Mark the lesson practised when *you* feel a repeatable hold seated or standing—StandardTune does not grade posture, chin pressure, or shoulder-rest setup.

## Lesson `viola-bow-open-cg`

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **id** | `viola-bow-open-cg` |
| **title** | Bow on Open C and G |
| **description** | Draw a calm bow on the two lowest open strings before adding fingers. |
| **minutes** | 6 |
| **outcome** | Produce several clear open C3 and open G3 tones with a simple whole-bow or mid-bow stroke. |
| **practice** | In Tune, select viola / Standard C (C3 G3 D4 A4). Soften the left hand on the neck without pressing fingers down. On open C, draw the bow parallel to the bridge at a moderate contact point; listen for a steady tone, then rest. Repeat on open G. Alternate C and G slowly. Use the tuner only as a pitch hint if you wish—judge bow noise, scratch, and smoothness yourself. |
| **readyWhen** | You can name open C as lower than open G and play a few strokes on each that sound intentional, without using left-hand fingers. Self-check: the app does not grade the bow. |

### Sections

#### Bow the open C string first

The viola’s lowest string is sounding C3—below the violin’s open G. Keep fingers off the fingerboard. Hold the bow so the stick sits in a soft hand with flexible fingers; the frog stays near the palm area you already use for bowing, without a death grip. Place the hair on open C roughly between the bridge and the end of the fingerboard, hair flat enough to speak, and draw a slow stroke parallel to the bridge. Listen for scrape versus a centered sound; if it scratches, lighten weight or move slightly toward the fingerboard, then try again. One clear open C matters more than many frantic bows. Optional: glance at the viola tuner for C3, then ignore the needle and listen—pitch alone is not a bow grade.

#### Add open G, still no fingers

When open C feels predictable, move to the neighbouring open G3 with the same calm stroke. Keep the left hand soft on the neck as a guide only; do not stop any string with a fingertip. Alternate C and G so your ear learns the fifth between them on *this* instrument, not from a violin map. Watch that the bow stays level as you change strings on the larger viola body—the arm travels a little farther than on violin. Stop while tone is still clear. StandardTune may match an open pitch in the Tuner; it cannot certify straight bow, contact point, or hair tilt. Ready means *you* hear intentional open C and G—not a microphone pass on bow technique.

## Lesson `viola-alto-landmarks`

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **id** | `viola-alto-landmarks` |
| **title** | Alto Clef Open-String Names |
| **description** | Link alto-clef landmarks to open C, G, D, and A by name only. |
| **minutes** | 5 |
| **outcome** | Name the four open strings and state that viola music uses alto clef, not violin treble. |
| **practice** | Say aloud: lowest to highest — C, G, D, A. Point to each string on the instrument as you name it. Recall that alto clef centres written middle C on the middle line; open C sits below that landmark, then G, D, and A rise by fifths. Do **not** copy staff worksheets or exam extracts—names and instrument only. Optionally confirm each open pitch once in the viola tuner. |
| **readyWhen** | You can name C3–G3–D4–A4 in order, say “alto clef,” and explain why a violin treble chart is the wrong reading map. Self-check only. |

### Sections

#### Alto clef is the viola reading home

Beginner violin material lives in treble clef; beginner viola material lives in **alto clef**. That is not a cosmetic difference: the same printed staff position does not mean the same sounding pitch across the two instruments. Remember one verbal landmark only—middle C written on the middle line of the alto staff—then relate your open strings by name: open C is the low string below that centre, then open G, open D, and open A ascending by fifths (C3 G3 D4 A4). Do not paste or redraw published ABRSM exercises, method-book staves, or violin note charts into practice. This lesson is vocabulary: clef name plus four open-string names tied to the instrument in your hands.

#### Four open names beat a violin fingering chart

A violin fingering chart assumes G–D–A–E and treble landmarks; using it on viola places fingers and note names on the wrong strings. Stay on open strings. Touch each string lightly with the bow or a soft pluck and say its letter: C, G, D, A from thickest/lowest to thinnest/highest. If you use the tuner, choose the viola profile so targets are C3 G3 D4 A4—not violin. No left-hand stopping yet; open-before-fingers still applies. The app will not grade reading or fingered intonation, and there is no staff-following drill here—only names you can say while pointing at the strings. Mark practised when those four names and “alto clef” are automatic without borrowing a violin map.
