# Beginner 5-string banjo sequence (StandardTune)

## Scope (not a path)

Tuner profile `banjo-5` is **experimental**. Learn has **no banjo learning instrument** and **no banjo curriculum path**. This brief specifies **at most three** optional, short, **SELF-CHECK** lessons for a beginner five-string banjo in open G. Do **not** insert these ids into guitar, classical, electric, or bass paths as required steps, unit gates, or progress prerequisites. They may appear only as optional extras (or stay out of the catalogue until product chooses to surface them).

**Right-hand identity for this sequence:** three-finger **thumb–index–middle** picking (fingerstyle rolls), seated with the banjo supported so both hands are free. **Clawhammer (frailing) is a different right-hand technique and is NOT this path**—do not mix clawhammer down-strokes, brushes, or drop-thumb into these lesson copy or drills.

**Deering-aligned beginner standards (do not copy Deering songs or published roll tablature verbatim):** open G tuning written **gDGBD** (short high **g** drone on the 5th string, then D G B D on the longer strings); first open chords **G**, **C**, and **D7**; then a **slow forward roll** practiced with a metronome. This brief covers tune → G strum → one original eight-note roll only. C and D7 are named as the next chord landmarks after G; they are not separate scored lessons here.

**Scoring limit:** StandardTune **cannot pitch-score banjo today**. Every lesson below is **SELF-CHECK only**—ear, hand observation, and (for tuning) the experimental tuner needle. Do not attach a fake microphone drill, guitar-string map, or pretended banjo pitch matcher.

**Tuning (reference only):**

| Profile | Open strings (as listed in-app) | Common label |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `banjo-5` / `banjo-5-open-g` | G4, D3, G3, B3, D4 (re-entrant 5th) | Open G · **gDGBD** |

String numbers for learners: **5th** = short drone (highest pitch, nearest the floor when held upright is *not* the mental model—on a banjo the 5th is the short string near the peghead); **4th–1st** = D G B D from thickest long string to thinnest.

## Lesson `banjo-open-g-fifth`

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **id** | `banjo-open-g-fifth` |
| **title** | Open G and the Short Fifth |
| **description** | Tune a five-string banjo to open G (gDGBD) with the experimental banjo-5 profile, then find and name the short 5th-string drone by ear. Self-check only—no scored drill. |
| **minutes** | 6 |
| **outcome** | Identify open G tuning by the gDGBD layout and locate the short 5th string as a high drone next to the longer fourth-string D. |
| **practice** | In Tune, select the experimental 5-string banjo profile and Open G. Pluck each long string once (4th→1st: D G B D), then pluck the short 5th alone and let it ring. Compare 5th (high g) to 1st (high D) and to 3rd (G). Mute neighbours if the needle jumps. Judge match by ear and tuner—not a lesson pitch score. |
| **readyWhen** | You can point to the short 5th string, name it as the high g drone, and say whether it needs to rise or fall relative to the Open G target without guessing which peg is which. |

### Sections

#### Tune open G on the experimental profile

Five-string open G is the Deering-style beginner home: written **gDGBD**. In StandardTune, choose instrument **5-String Banjo** (`banjo-5`, marked experimental) and tuning **Open G**. Work one string at a time: start the listener, select the labeled string, pluck once, and make tiny peg changes. The long strings from thickest to thinnest are D3, G3, B3, and D4; the short string is the re-entrant high G4 drone. Phones often hear banjo overtones, so if the display leaps an octave, mute every other string, move closer to the head, and try again. Do not trust a guitar EADGBE preset or a four-string bass map—those are different instruments. Stop if you are unsure which machine belongs to which string; a wrong turn can snap a string. When all five targets feel settled to *you* and the needle, you are tuned enough to learn—there is no banjo pitch-score to certify it.

#### Find the short fifth as a drone

The 5th string is shorter than the others and usually ends at a peg on the side of the neck. Pluck only that short string and listen for a bright high **g** that sits above the open long strings. Next pluck the 4th (low D) and the 3rd (G) so you can hear how the drone relates to the open G chord that all five strings form when fretting hands stay off. Say aloud: “fifth string, high g, drone.” Your fretting hand should not be supporting the neck; let the strap or lap hold the banjo so you can mute and unmute the 5th on purpose. This lesson is a landmark hunt, not a song. Mark it practised when *you* can find the short string in the dark and recognize its pitch family—StandardTune will not award a banjo pitch match.

## Lesson `banjo-strum-g`

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **id** | `banjo-strum-g` |
| **title** | Strum Open G |
| **description** | Form the open G chord on five-string banjo and brush slow downstrokes so every intended string rings. Self-check only—no scored drill. |
| **minutes** | 6 |
| **outcome** | Play a clear open G (all open strings in gDGBD) with even, unhurried downstrokes and hear when a string stays muted or buzzes. |
| **practice** | Leave fretting fingers off for open G. At a slow spoken count, brush down across the long strings; optionally include the 5th drone if your brush reaches it cleanly. Listen string-by-string. Peek at C and D7 diagrams only as “next shapes,” then return to G. No microphone lesson score. |
| **readyWhen** | You can play several slow bars of open G downs, name the chord as G, and fix one dead string by ear without rushing. |

### Sections

#### Open G is the home chord

On open G banjo, fretting nothing already gives a G major sonority across gDGBD—that is why beginners start here. Sit balanced, raise the neck slightly, and let the right hand brush from the 4th string toward the 1st with a small wrist motion (thumb or fingernail brush is fine for this lesson; save roll fingers for the next). Count “one-two-three-four” and land one downstroke per beat at a tempo you could speak comfortably. After each brush, listen: does the low D speak? Does the B sound clear? If one string is dull, pluck it alone, check that nothing is touching it, then brush again. This is **SELF-CHECK**—the app has no banjo chord detector. Keep volume modest; a banjo head is loud in a small room.

#### G first; C and D7 come next, not today as homework piles

Deering-style beginner harmony usually adds **C** and **D7** after G feels solid. For this session, stay on open G until downs feel even. Only after several clean G bars, glance at how C and D7 will later change a few fretted notes on the long strings while the 5th often stays a drone—then put those shapes away. Do not chase a full three-chord song or copy published song charts. The success test is simple: you can strum G, hear all the notes you meant to hear, and know that C and D7 are the next chord names on this instrument family. Clawhammer brushes are a different right-hand path; if you are “bump-ditty” frailing, you have left this sequence—return to calm downstrokes or move to the roll lesson’s thumb–index–middle language instead.

## Lesson `banjo-eight-forward`

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **id** | `banjo-eight-forward` |
| **title** | Slow Eight-Note Forward Roll |
| **description** | Play one original eight-note forward roll with thumb, index, and middle on open G, slowly with a metronome. Self-check only—no scored drill and no copied song licks. |
| **minutes** | 8 |
| **outcome** | Repeat an even eight-note thumb–index–middle forward roll on open strings at a slow click without tensing the picking hand. |
| **practice** | Metronome ~60 BPM, one note per click. On open G, play this original pattern once per two bars: thumb on 3rd (G), index on 2nd (B), middle on 1st (D), thumb on 5th (high g), index on 2nd (B), middle on 1st (D), thumb on 4th (low D), index on 2nd (B). Loop; listen for equal spacing. Not a famous tune—judge evenness yourself. |
| **readyWhen** | You can play the eight-note cycle at least four times in a row at one steady slow tempo, naming thumb / index / middle as you go, with no scramble to “keep up.” |

### Sections

#### Forward roll means thumb then index then middle, looping forward

A forward roll is a repeating right-hand order that moves “forward” through thumb → index → middle (and back into thumb) rather than alternating randomly. Rest the fingertip pads (or short nails) close to the strings above the head; keep the motion tiny. This lesson’s pattern is an **original practice figure**, not a transcription of a recorded Scruggs lick and not tablature lifted from a published method song. In words only: (1) thumb plays the open **3rd** string, (2) index the open **2nd**, (3) middle the open **1st**, (4) thumb the short **5th** drone, (5) index **2nd**, (6) middle **1st**, (7) thumb open **4th**, (8) index **2nd**. That is eight notes; then start again at (1). Stay on open G so the fretting hand can relax. If clawhammer instincts appear (whole-hand down-stroke on the beat), stop and reset to individual finger plucks—clawhammer is **not** this path.

#### Metronome first, melody never the goal today

Set Tempo (or any click) near **60 BPM** and play **one roll note per click**. The goal is even time and clear attacks, not speed or a recognizable breakdown tune. After one clean eight, rest a bar and breathe; then loop. Listen for a note that rushes ahead of the click or a thumb that stomps louder than the fingers—soften that finger instead of pushing tempo. Because StandardTune cannot pitch-score banjo, green lessons elsewhere do not apply: *you* decide when the roll sounds steady. Do not paste famous recorded fills, named Scruggs vocabulary from albums, or book tab measure-for-measure into this practice. When four slow cycles feel boringly even, you are ready—not when the pattern sounds like a song you have heard on a record.
